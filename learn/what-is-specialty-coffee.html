The Basics

What is Specialty Coffee?

Not all coffee is created equal. Specialty coffee is the top tier of the coffee world — and once you taste the difference, there's no going back.

It starts with a score.

Specialty coffee is officially defined as coffee that scores 80 points or above on a 100-point scale, as evaluated by a certified Q Grader — a trained sensory professional. The score is based on attributes like aroma, flavor, aftertaste, acidity, body, balance, and the absence of defects.

Anything below 80 is considered commercial-grade coffee — the kind most commonly used in instant blends, canned coffee, and mass-market brands. It is not bad, but it is not specialty.

"Specialty coffee scored between 80–84.99 is considered 'Very Good.' 85–89.99 is 'Excellent.' 90 and above is 'Outstanding' — extremely rare."

The full chain matters.

A high score alone is not enough. Specialty coffee is defined by a commitment to quality at every step of the supply chain:

  • Farmers — selective hand-picking of only ripe cherries, careful processing
  • Exporters & importers — proper storage, green grading, fair contracts
  • Roasters — small-batch roasting designed to highlight the bean's origin character
  • Baristas — precise brewing to bring out the intended flavor

Break the chain at any point — bad storage, over-roasting, careless brewing — and even a 90-point bean can taste ordinary.

Why does it taste different?

Commercial coffee is often blended from many origins to produce a consistent, neutral flavor. Specialty coffee celebrates the opposite: the unique characteristics of a single origin, a single farm, or even a single lot.

Altitude, soil, rainfall, processing method, and variety all leave their fingerprint in the cup. A highland Arabica from Lanao del Sur will taste nothing like one from Ethiopia or Colombia — and that's the point.

The Philippines and specialty coffee.

The Philippines has four commercially grown coffee species: Arabica, Robusta, Liberica (Barako), and Excelsa. The specialty coffee movement here is still young, but the country's highland regions — Benguet, Bukidnon, Mt. Apo, Lanao del Sur — are producing beans that are beginning to attract serious attention from the global coffee community.

Cooperatives like the St. Mary of Downtown Farmers (SMD) in Amai Manabilang, Lanao del Sur are at the forefront of this movement — growing with care, processing with intention, and proving that world-class coffee can come from Philippine soil.

"Specialty coffee is not a price point or a brand. It is a standard — earned by every person who touched the bean before it reached your cup."
