Coffee Species

What is Excelsa?

Most of the world drinks Arabica or Robusta. A small slice — mostly in Southeast Asia — drinks something else. This is the third coffee.

The third coffee.

Of the roughly 130 known species of coffee, only three are commercially significant: Arabica, Robusta, and Liberica. Excelsa was once classified as its own species — Coffea excelsa — but was reclassified in 2006 as a variety of Coffea liberica. Practically, most coffee professionals still talk about Excelsa as its own thing, because it tastes and behaves differently from typical Liberica.

Excelsa makes up less than 1% of global coffee production. The vast majority of people who consider themselves "into coffee" have never knowingly tasted it.

Where Excelsa grows.

Excelsa is largely a Southeast Asian story. It is found in Vietnam, India, parts of Indonesia, southern China, parts of Africa — and the Philippines, where it has been grown for generations. The plant prefers mid-elevations (around 600–1,200 meters) and is more heat-tolerant than Arabica, which makes it well-suited to lowland tropical climates.

The Excelsa tree itself is striking — taller than most coffee plants, with larger leaves and longer cherries. The beans are characteristically more elongated and pointed than Arabica's flatter oval shape, which is one of the easiest visual cues to identify it in a roasted form.

How it tastes.

This is where Excelsa earns its reputation. Done well, it produces a cup that is unmistakably its own — not better or worse than Arabica, just different.

  • Fruity — often tart and tropical: jackfruit, dried mango, stone fruit, sometimes citrus.
  • Spiced — gentle warmth on the finish; some cups read as cinnamon, clove, or anise.
  • Syrupy body — a heavier, rounder mouthfeel than most Arabica filter coffees.
  • Sweet aftertaste — molasses, brown sugar, sometimes a slight cola-like quality on the back end.
  • Lower brightness — Excelsa is not as acidic as a high-altitude Arabica. The complexity sits in the body and finish, not the front.

Roast level changes the conversation significantly. A light roast pulls forward the fruit and the spice. A medium-to-dark roast leans into chocolate, molasses, and a deeper sweetness — closer to what most Filipino drinkers grew up with.

Excelsa in the Philippines.

Coffee has been grown in the Philippines since the 1700s, and Excelsa has been part of that story for more than a century. While Arabica gets most of the modern specialty attention, Excelsa quietly persists in places where Arabica won't grow well — particularly in Mindanao, where the climate and altitude suit it.

Today you'll find Excelsa in pockets of Davao del Sur, South Cotabato, Bukidnon, and Sultan Kudarat. Some of it goes into commercial blends and never gets named. A small amount finds its way to specialty roasters who treat it as a single origin — letting the cup speak for itself instead of hiding it inside a blend.

The Philippine Excelsa story is still early. Most farmers grew it because it survived where other species didn't, not because they were chasing a flavor profile. As specialty coffee grows here, those same trees — now decades old — are being rediscovered as something distinctive rather than something secondary.

Why aslom roasts Excelsa.

Our launch bean — Alta Vista Excelsa — is sourced direct from farmer Boy in Brgy. Alta Vista, Bansalan, Davao del Sur. We chose it for three reasons:

  • It's local. Davao-grown, single-farm, single-lot. We can name the place. We can name the farmer.
  • It's rare. You won't find it on most cafe menus. That alone makes it worth showing up for.
  • It rewards a careful roast. Light-roasted for filter, the fruit and spice come forward, the syrupy body holds the cup together, the finish is sweet without being heavy. It's not a coffee you forget.

Excelsa is not a replacement for Arabica. It's its own thing. If you've spent years drinking specialty Arabica, this will feel different — not lesser, just different. That's the point.
