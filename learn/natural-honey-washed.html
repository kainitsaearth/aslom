Processing

Natural, Honey & Washed

Before a coffee bean becomes a roastable seed, it has to be separated from its fruit. How that happens — and how long it takes — shapes the flavor in your cup more than almost anything else.

What is coffee processing?

A coffee cherry looks like a small red or yellow fruit. Inside is the seed — what we call the coffee bean. Surrounding that seed are several layers: the outer skin (pulp), a sticky mucilage layer, and a thin parchment.

Processing is the method of removing these layers to get to the dry, stable green bean that can be shipped, roasted, and brewed. The three main methods — natural, honey, and washed — differ in how much of the fruit remains on the bean during the drying stage, and that difference is enormous in terms of flavor.

Natural

Fruity · Winey · Sweet

The whole cherry is dried intact. The bean absorbs sugars and flavors from the fruit over weeks. The most complex and funky of the three.

Honey

Sweet · Balanced · Smooth

The skin is removed but some or all of the mucilage (the "honey") is left on the bean during drying. A middle ground between natural and washed.

Washed

Clean · Bright · Crisp

All fruit and mucilage is removed before drying. The cup reflects the bean's origin character most directly — terroir in its clearest form.

The Natural process.

In the natural (or dry) process, freshly harvested cherries are spread out on raised drying beds or patios and left to dry in the sun — sometimes for 3–6 weeks. The bean ferments gently inside the fruit the entire time, absorbing the sugars and aromatic compounds from the pulp.

The result is a coffee that is heavy in body, low in acidity, and intensely fruity — blueberry, strawberry, tropical fruit, and wine-like fermentation notes are common. It is the oldest processing method in the world, originally used in Ethiopia and Yemen.

The risk: if not carefully managed, natural-processed coffee can develop over-fermented, sour, or unclean flavors. Done well, it produces some of the most exciting and complex coffees in the world.

"A well-executed natural process is like the difference between fresh fruit and fruit jam — intense, sweet, and deeply aromatic."

The Honey process.

The honey process originated in Costa Rica and has become popular throughout Central America and Southeast Asia. The outer skin is mechanically removed, but the sticky mucilage layer is left on the bean during drying.

The amount of mucilage left on the bean determines the intensity of the effect — and this is where the naming gets creative:

  • Yellow Honey — most mucilage removed, shortest drying time, lightest cup
  • Red Honey — moderate mucilage, medium drying, more sweetness
  • Black Honey — maximum mucilage retained, longest drying, closest to natural in flavor

Honey-processed coffees tend to be syrupy, sweet, and smooth — with more body than washed and more clarity than natural. They are often described as approachable and crowd-pleasing.

The Washed process.

In the washed (or wet) process, the pulp is removed immediately after harvesting, and the beans are fermented in water tanks to break down and rinse away the remaining mucilage. After fermentation, the clean beans are dried on raised beds.

Washed coffees are the clearest expression of terroir — the soil, altitude, microclimate, and variety. Without layers of fermentation flavor from the fruit, what you taste is the bean itself. The cup is typically bright, clean, and high in acidity, with well-defined and complex flavor notes.

Most Ethiopian specialty coffees — the benchmark for what high-quality coffee can taste like — are washed processed. The Yirgacheffe region is famous worldwide for its washed coffees with jasmine, bergamot, and lemon notes.

"If the natural process is a painting with bold, expressive brushstrokes, the washed process is a precise line drawing — every detail of the bean's origin made visible."

Which process is better?

None of them. Each serves a different purpose and delivers a different experience. The best process is the one that is executed skillfully and that matches the flavor profile the farmer or roaster is trying to achieve.

As a coffee drinker, the most useful thing you can do is try all three and develop your own preference. Pay attention to how each process feels in your mouth, not just how it tastes. The difference in body and mouthfeel is often what makes the lasting impression.
